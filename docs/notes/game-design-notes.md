# Adventure House Design Notes

## Purpose

This file is a local memo for the current game direction. It is meant to preserve intent, vocabulary, and unresolved questions so later implementation does not drift away from the original fantasy.

## Core Fantasy

The player does not go on adventures.

The player runs a foothold in an adventurer village: a shop, guild-adjacent business, or service hub. The player influences adventurers indirectly through tasks, gifts, services, relationships, and infrastructure.

The fun comes from:

- shaping people who have their own goals
- seeing delayed and incomplete consequences
- turning fragments of information into strategy
- building a business that can profit from other people's adventures without controlling them completely

## Player Role

The player is not an omniscient commander and not a direct combatant.

The player should feel like:

- a manager of local services
- a broker of opportunities
- a processor of value
- a relationship builder
- a person trying to influence fate from the village side of the world

## Information Rule

This is a key design principle.

The player should not see the full adventure process in real time. The player is not supposed to have god-view access to every battle, route choice, or event.

Most "mainline" progress should come from fragments collected from adventurers and returned objects, such as:

- spoken reports after return
- injuries and equipment condition
- letters or notes
- relics and unusual drops
- rumors brought by travelers
- traces of failed expeditions

The player must still get feedback. Hidden information should create mystery, not confusion. The rule is:

- no full live knowledge
- no guaranteed full report
- yes to delayed, partial, biased feedback

## Core Loop

1. The player runs a village foothold and maintains cash flow.
2. The player publishes tasks, provides services, gives gifts, and builds relationships.
3. Adventurers decide what to take based on reward, personality, needs, risk tolerance, and relationship.
4. Adventurers leave and act off-screen.
5. They return with materials, stories, injuries, clues, or nothing at all.
6. The player interprets those fragments, adjusts strategy, and upgrades the foothold.

## Adventurers

Adventurers are not robots. They should have their own reasons to act.

Possible motivations:

- making a living
- seeking danger or glory
- repaying debts
- funding personal goals
- gathering knowledge
- chasing rumors, revenge, or status

They may take a player's task because it overlaps with what they already want to do, not because they exist to obey.

## Relationship Structure

Relationship should increase influence, not remove autonomy.

Suggested progression:

- Stranger: can only interact through public tasks
- Regular: more likely to accept fitting tasks
- Trusted: can receive directed requests and personal gifts
- Core Partner: long-term cooperation, stronger influence, more detailed feedback

High relationship should not turn characters into mindless employees. It should mean the player gains access, trust, priority, and negotiation power.

## Growth Philosophy

Adventurer growth should involve tradeoffs.

The player should not be able to raise every character into an all-purpose perfect unit. The game should favor "you can go this way, or that way" over "you must do X before Y."

Implications:

- growth paths should branch
- investment should force choices
- strengths should create weaknesses or opportunity cost
- gifts and support should nudge development, not fully determine it

The player influences growth, but adventurers also manage themselves. They may decide how to spend money, what gear to favor, what jobs to prioritize, and what kind of life to pursue.

## Task Types

Tasks currently fall into two main categories.

### 1. Economic Tasks

These are practical supply tasks.

Examples:

- gather wood
- collect herbs
- bring back food ingredients
- deliver common materials

Purpose:

- maintain stock
- feed shops and buildings
- stabilize the local economy
- support the player's basic cash flow

### 2. Directional Tasks

These influence behavior and world progress more directly.

Examples:

- prioritize exploring a specific area
- investigate a rumor
- escort a scholar
- avoid a high-risk route
- search for traces of a missing party

Purpose:

- steer adventurer behavior
- create fragmented "main story" progression
- let the player act strategically without direct control

## Why Adventurers Accept Tasks

This is a foundational economy rule.

If an adventurer can obtain something more profitably alone, a task must still offer value in another form. Task rewards are not only "payment for labor." The player foothold should provide a package of advantages:

- immediate cash flow
- lower selling friction
- reliable buyers
- risk sharing
- access to food, treatment, lodging, tools, and repairs
- social connection and trust
- future opportunities

The player's economic value is not "being the only one who can do anything." It is being better at converting raw returns into stable, higher-value outcomes.

Short version:

Adventurers bring opportunities back from the world. The player turns those opportunities into reliable and amplified value.

## Business and Processing

The player needs independent economic leverage, otherwise the whole loop becomes "pay everyone forever."

The village foothold should be able to:

- buy or receive raw materials
- process them into better goods
- provide services that adventurers cannot efficiently self-provide
- profit from infrastructure, logistics, and customer flow

Early examples:

- tavern or food counter
- quest board
- storage and procurement

Later examples:

- clinic
- smithy
- inn
- information desk

These buildings should not be isolated money buttons. They should exist to support the core fantasy of influencing adventurers and profiting from the ecosystem around them.

## Mainline Progression

The "main story" should mostly be discovered through fragments, not clean quest logs.

The player may start with one or two known hooks, but most future threads should emerge from:

- returned reports
- unusual mission outcomes
- relics
- missing adventurers
- market anomalies
- repeated references to the same place, faction, or event

This keeps the player in-world and prevents them from feeling like an external narrator.

## Reset / Prestige Direction

This is still unresolved, but current thinking should avoid full hard resets that erase the emotional value of specific adventurers.

A better fit is likely some form of:

- village inheritance
- era progression
- legacy systems
- partial reset of short-term resources with retention of long-term knowledge and status

Things that could reset:

- money
- inventory
- current contracts
- short-term market state

Things that could partially remain:

- reputation
- blueprints
- discovered information
- relic collection
- records of notable adventurers

## MVP Boundary

The first playable version should validate the core fantasy, not the entire world.

Recommended MVP scope:

- a small set of adventurers with visible differences
- basic relationship values
- economic tasks
- directional tasks
- one core business building
- simple material-to-product conversion
- return reports with partial information

Avoid for early versions:

- full market simulation
- deep correspondence systems
- complex prestige loops
- large-scale population simulation
- too many buildings at once

## Current Open Questions

- What is the clearest long-term player objective?
- How much agency should adventurers retain in spending and self-development?
- What minimum information is enough for the player to make good decisions without breaking the mystery?
- How punishing should failure be: financial loss, injury, disappearance, relationship damage, reputation damage?
- How many named recurring adventurers are needed before relationship play feels meaningful?
- What exact form should legacy/reset take so that replay speed increases without invalidating emotional attachment?

## Working Principles

- The player influences, but does not fully command.
- The world should feel alive because characters have their own motives.
- Mystery is good; missing feedback is bad.
- Growth should require tradeoffs.
- Economy systems should support the relationship and task loop, not distract from it.
- New systems should layer onto the core fantasy instead of becoming separate minigames.
